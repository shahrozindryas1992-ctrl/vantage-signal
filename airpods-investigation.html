What if your AirPods could see what you see?

A leaked Apple demonstration appears to show AirPods with outward-facing cameras being used for Visual Intelligence. The product is not currently shipping, but the leak exposes a bigger question: what changes when an everyday device becomes another set of eyes?

The interesting part of this story is not really the AirPods. It is the direction of the technology.

For years, cameras have lived in obvious places: phones, laptops, doorbells and security systems. You can usually see them. You can choose whether to point them at something.

A camera built into an earbud is different. It sits in a device people already wear while walking, shopping, working, commuting and talking. If the camera is there to give an AI assistant visual context, the device does not need to behave like a traditional camera to create new privacy questions.

The leak

In August 2026, references to unreleased camera-equipped AirPods appeared in Apple's software. A video found in a macOS release candidate showed a user wearing the earbuds while using a feature described as Visual Intelligence. The demonstration suggested that the earbuds could capture what the wearer was looking at and use that visual information to help an AI assistant understand the environment.

Macworld reported that the product identifier B790 appeared in the software and that the demonstration video was included in the macOS release candidate. Other reporting found additional software frameworks suggesting that the cameras were being developed for AI functions rather than ordinary photography. Macworld's reporting and The Verge's analysis both describe the leaked demonstration.

Important: These are not a publicly released Apple product. Apple has not announced camera-equipped AirPods. Later reporting indicates the project has been pushed to 2027, so the hardware and features described in leaked software may still change.

These are not necessarily “cameras” in the way we think of cameras

That distinction matters.

Reporting on the leaked software suggests the sensors are intended to provide visual information to AI features rather than act as a conventional photo or video camera. Macworld reported that the in-development system appeared to use active and passive visual modes, with low-resolution background sensing and higher-resolution captures when a visual request is made.

That could eventually allow an assistant to understand things such as objects, surroundings, people or the context of what the wearer is looking at.

And that is where the privacy question begins

Imagine walking through a coffee shop wearing them.

Someone sits across from you with a laptop open. Another person walks past. A document is lying on a table. A badge is visible. A child's face appears in the background.

The wearer may be asking the AI a completely ordinary question.

But the sensor is still looking at the world around them.

The consent problem is bigger than the user

Traditional privacy controls often focus on the person who owns the device.

But ambient sensing creates a second group: the people around the device.

They did not buy it. They may not know it is there. They may not know what the device is capable of seeing or what happens to the information after it is captured.

This is the same broader issue raised by other AI wearables: the person operating the device and the person being observed are not necessarily the same person.

Why this matters for cybersecurity too

Privacy is only one part of the equation.

Once a device can continuously sense its environment, the security value of that data changes. Visual information can reveal physical locations, screens, documents, identities, workplace layouts and other contextual information that traditional audio-only earbuds could never observe.

That creates questions around data minimization, processing location, retention, access controls, account compromise and what an attacker could learn if the surrounding data were exposed.

The bigger signal

The important trend is not “Apple might put cameras in AirPods.”

The bigger trend is that AI assistants are becoming increasingly dependent on multimodal context: what we say, what we see, where we are and what is happening around us.

As that happens, sensors stop being isolated features. They become part of an intelligence layer around the user.

And once AI can see through the devices we already wear, privacy is no longer only about what we intentionally photograph.

It becomes about what our devices can perceive while we are simply living our lives.

What we know — and what we don't

We know

  • Apple software leaks showed an unreleased AirPods project with cameras.
  • A leaked demonstration showed Visual Intelligence being used with the device.
  • Software references suggest visual sensing was being developed for AI-related functions.

We don't know

  • Exactly what final hardware will ship.
  • Exactly what data will be processed locally or remotely.
  • How long visual data would be retained, if at all.
  • What final privacy controls and indicators Apple would provide.

Our takeaway

There is no need to assume that camera-equipped earbuds will automatically become a privacy disaster. They could also create legitimate accessibility, navigation and contextual-AI benefits.

But the design question needs to be asked before the technology becomes normal.

When an AI assistant gets eyes, whose world is it looking at?
