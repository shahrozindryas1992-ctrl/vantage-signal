The human decisions attackers look for.

Attackers do not need every employee to make a mistake. They need one decision that gives the next step of an intrusion a chance to work.

The “human element” is often described as if employees are the weak link. That framing is too simple.

People are not machines. They work under deadlines, answer messages on phones, approve routine requests, reset passwords, move files, onboard colleagues and make judgment calls with incomplete information.

Attackers understand that environment. Their objective is often not to fool someone with an obviously bad email. It is to create a situation where a reasonable person makes a reasonable decision — at the wrong moment.

The attack path starts before the click

The 2026 Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report found that mobile social-engineering attacks were succeeding at higher rates than traditional email phishing, while vulnerability exploitation had become the leading initial access vector overall. The important signal is that attackers are not abandoning people; they are changing how they approach them.

A text message from a manager. A voice call that sounds familiar. A request that appears to come from finance. A login prompt delivered at exactly the moment someone expects to be working.

The useful question is not “Why did the employee click?” It is “What conditions made the attacker’s request look normal?”

Attackers look for decisions, not stupidity

Consider a few ordinary decisions: approve a new bank account, share a document, install a browser extension, reset a password, grant access to a contractor, or send a file to a colleague.

None is inherently dangerous.

The risk appears when identity, urgency and authority are manipulated at the same time.

Why policies alone do not solve it

A security policy can say “never share your password.” That is necessary. It does not address the harder questions: Who can approve a payment? How is an unusual request verified? What happens when a former employee still has access? Who can add a new administrator? Can a recruiter paste a candidate's sensitive information into an AI tool?

Human risk lives in these workflows.

The phone changed the equation

Mobile messaging collapses context. An employee can receive a convincing request while walking between meetings, driving a commute, or dealing with a customer. The interface is optimized for speed, not investigation.

Verizon's 2026 findings are a reminder that awareness programs need to follow attackers into the channels people actually use.

What organizations can change

  • Create verification steps for high-impact requests.
  • Reduce unnecessary standing privileges.
  • Make reporting suspicious requests easy and non-punitive.
  • Review onboarding and offboarding as security processes.
  • Train employees around realistic scenarios, not just phishing examples.
  • Measure whether controls make the safe decision easier.

Our takeaway

The goal is not to build a workforce that never makes mistakes. That is impossible.

The goal is to design an organization where one mistake is less likely to become an incident.

Good security does not ask people to be perfect. It gives them safer decisions to make.
