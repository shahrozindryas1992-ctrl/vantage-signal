AI / HUMAN RISK / DATA PRIVACY

When an employee puts company data into an AI tool.

The quiet AI security problem is not always a sophisticated attack. Sometimes it is ordinary work moving sensitive information into a system the organization does not fully control.

A spreadsheet, customer email or internal document can cross an organization's security boundary with one paste.

The employee may not be trying to bypass security. They may be trying to finish a task faster.

The new data-loss channel

Generative AI has created a workplace workflow that traditional security programs were not designed to observe: conversational data transfer.

An employee copies a contract into an AI assistant to summarize it. A recruiter pastes candidate information to improve a job description. A developer uploads code to debug an error. A finance employee asks the model to explain a budget.

Each action can be useful. Each can also move sensitive information into a different processing environment.

This is why “just don't use AI” fails

Employees are already using AI because it can materially improve productivity. Blocking every tool often pushes usage into less visible channels.

Recent research on “digital shadow AI” describes how efficiency incentives can increase AI use and data sharing while uncertainty about privacy and governance reduces trust. Verizon's 2026 DBIR also reported a sharp rise in unapproved “shadow AI” use.

The governance question is not only which AI tool employees use. It is what information the organization allows to enter each tool, under which controls, and with what visibility.

Meta shows why the issue can become internal too

In 2026, Meta's Model Capability Initiative drew employee backlash over plans involving internal worker data such as keystrokes and mouse movements for AI training. Reporting said the program was later revised toward an opt-in model after concerns about privacy and access.

The lesson is broader than one company: employees are data subjects too. A company's AI strategy can create privacy risk even when the data never leaves the corporate environment.

What a practical AI policy should define

  • Approved AI tools and accounts.
  • Data that may never be entered into AI systems.
  • Rules for customer, employee, financial and confidential information.
  • Whether AI-generated work requires human review.
  • Who approves new AI tools.
  • How incidents involving AI data disclosure are reported.
  • How AI use is covered during onboarding and offboarding.

The control that matters most

Employees need a simple answer to a simple question: “Can I put this into AI?”

If the answer requires reading a 40-page policy every time, people will guess.

A useful program turns the policy into clear categories: public, internal, confidential, restricted. Then it gives employees approved tools and examples for each category.

Our takeaway

AI data risk is often presented as a technology problem. In practice, it is a people, policy and workflow problem.

The safest organization is not the one that tells employees never to use AI. It is the one that makes safe AI use easier than unsafe AI use.
